Solving the 1950 cipher meant to test life after death

By Colin Reilly October 4, 2026

Code and data Deutsche Fassung

Solved

Confirmed by Klaus Schmeh on October 4, 2026

Hier bin ich. Totsiens. T.E.W.

“Here I am” in German, “goodbye” in Afrikaans, and Wood’s initials. Written to be read after his death.

The key is the opening of the Lord’s Prayer in the 1912 Luther Bible, used with Robert Thouless’s 1948 word-sum system.

In 1950, a solicitor named T. E. Wood published a 21 letter cipher as a test of life after death. He planned to send the key back through a medium after he died. Seventy-six years later, we decrypted it. No séance required.

I · The cipher

The puzzle

His plan depended on keeping the key secret and if a medium supplied it after he passed, he hoped it would prove there was some kind of life after death.

The idea came from the psychologist Robert Thouless who published an article titled “A Test of Survival” (Proceedings of the Society for Psychical Research 48, 1948) with enciphered messages whose keys only he knew. One of them used a system meant to be unbreakable without the key:

  1. Pick a passage of text as the key, and skip any word that has already appeared.
  2. Turn each word into one letter. Add up its letters (A=1 to Z=26), divide by 26, and take the remainder (1 is A, 25 is Y, 0 is Z).
  3. Use that key letter to shift one message letter along the alphabet. A means no shift, B one place, C two, and so on, wrapping from Z back to A. Decoding shifts the other way.

Each key word supplies the shift for one message letter, so a 21-letter message needs 21 distinct key words. This was the method Wood used for his test of the afterlife.

Wood and his clues

In response to Thouless’s article, T. E. Wood, a solicitor born in Yorkshire in 1887 who had practiced in Burma, answered with his own message in “A Further Test for Survival” (Proceedings 49, 1950). He published FVAMI NTKFX XWATB OIZVV X and left the following clues:

  • He used Thouless’s method, with repeated words omitted.
  • The key passage “is in an accessible book”, in a foreign language.
  • The message “will not be in any one language”.
II · Searching books

Why it remained unsolved

If the key letters could be chosen freely, the same ciphertext could be made to say any message of the same length. A readable sentence alone would not tell you whether it was Wood’s, particularly one so short.

Where to begin

Wood had given another important clue: his key came from consecutive words in a published book. This made it possible to search a collection of books, trying each word as a starting point.

We used publicly available sources for the books and ran 100 known test ciphers to determine whether our code could recover a real message and correctly score it. On September 17, 2026, an initial search tested 28.6 million candidate book keys drawn from 129 books. Each decryption was scored by how much it resembled English. The correct message was the top-scoring result in 97 of 100 tests.

Finding the keys

The search first compared five-letter patterns, then checked how well the strongest candidates split into English words. It was a way to rank possible readings, with a clear weakness. Every reading was being judged as English.

Wood’s message produced no readable result. The search expanded from 129 books to 3,977 texts, adding 3,848 Project Gutenberg books in 14 languages and testing about 429 million candidate keys. It still found nothing convincing.

We ran the same search on 100 random strings of 21 letters. In 12 of those tests, it found a result that scored higher than anything it had found for Wood’s message. A bigger library had produced more tempting false leads, but no readable message.

III · Across languages

There was still Wood’s clue that the message “will not be in any one language”. The next search looked for words in seven languages and allowed the language to change from one word to the next. A quick check of letter patterns narrowed the candidates before the program tried splitting them into words. It favored common words and discouraged chopping a string into tiny fragments or switching languages repeatedly just to make the pieces fit.

We also needed a more targeted source. Wood said the key came from “an accessible book” in a foreign language. A Bible seemed plausible, so we added Bible translations in 19 languages to the search.

IV · The solution

The hit

The Bible run tried 26.7 million combinations of starting point and treatment of repeated words, for each of two versions of the arithmetic. The first pass took under three minutes.

Under Thouless’s rule, the top result came from the German Bible, starting at the sixth word of Matthew 6:9, the opening of the Lord’s Prayer. It decrypted to HIERBINICHTOTSIENSTEW.

Unser Vater in dem Himmel! Dein Name werde geheiligt. Dein Reich komme. Dein Wille geschehe auf Erden wie im Himmel. Unser täglich Brot gib uns …

The exact wording mattered. An earlier search had tried the German Lord’s Prayer beginning “Vater unser”, the familiar church wording. The Luther Bible’s “Unser Vater in dem Himmel” produces a different key from the very first word.

The key uses 21 distinct words, from “Unser” to “uns”. Four repeats are skipped (“Dein” twice, “Himmel” and “Unser” once each), and the ä in “täglich” is read as a.

All 21 letters decrypt correctly and re-encrypt to Wood’s original ciphertext.

Reading the message

The top result under Thouless’s rule was immediately recognizable once the letters were separated into words:

Hier bin ich. Totsiens. T.E.W.

“Hier bin ich” is German for “Here I am”. “Totsiens” is Afrikaans for “Goodbye”. T.E.W. are Wood’s initials.

Wood meant the message to be read after his death, once a medium had passed on the key. He announces himself, says goodbye and signs it. The key he would have sent begins “Our Father in heaven”.

V · Checking the result

How it was checked

Before sending the result to Klaus Schmeh, we checked the decryption in several ways.

Reproducing the decryption

A short standalone script, using no other project code, rebuilds the key from the prayer text, decrypts all 21 letters, and re-encrypts them back to Wood’s exact ciphertext. An independent AI agent, OpenAI’s Astra, independently rebuilt the decryption and reproduced all 21 letters. Its full review is available in the repository.

Original sources

Scans of Wood’s 1950 article and Thouless’s 1948 article confirmed the ciphertext, the method, and Wood’s clues word for word. They also confirmed that “A means no shift” comes from Thouless’s own text, not from a later guess.

Testing for chance matches

A follow-up ran about 854 million trial decryptions across 3,847 Gutenberg books and 19 Bibles, covering both versions of the arithmetic, with and without repeated words. It looked for 18 letters that split into listed words of 3 to 10 letters, followed by a three-letter signature.

With TEW there was exactly one match, and it was this message. With 500 random signatures in place of TEW, 7 of them (1.4%) also got a match, and the examples were word salad.

TOTSIENS was added to the word list after the solve, and the test was designed after the hit, so it shows rarity, not proof.

Checking for earlier solutions

Searches of Schmeh’s blog and its comments, Reddit’s r/codes, and the other 2026 solver logs found no earlier solution of Wood’s cryptogram. That is not proof that nobody got there first.

VI · Further findings

An error in the original example

While checking the method against the 1948 paper, we found an arithmetic slip in Thouless’s own worked example. It has been in print for 78 years, and we have not seen it pointed out anywhere.

To show how his system works, Thouless enciphered THERE IS NO DEATH with Hamlet’s “To be or not to be” speech as the key. He printed the key letters IGGWW BG PI VNWNU and the ciphertext BNKNA JYCWY RWGB.

The first 13 key letters are right. The 14th key word is “suffer”:

  • S+U+F+F+E+R = 19+21+6+6+5+18 = 75
  • 75 = 2 × 26 + 23, so the key letter should be W, the 23rd letter.
  • Thouless printed U, the 21st.

His printed ciphertext follows the printed U, so this is not a single misprint. The 14th plain letter is the final H of DEATH. Shifted by U it becomes B, which he printed. Shifted by the correct W it would be D. So the last group of his example should read RWGD, not RWGB.

Wood’s 21 key letters all agree with Thouless’s written rule, which is the method used to decrypt his message.

Telling the Society for Psychical Research

The key came from a search of books, not from a medium. Make of that what you will. What remains is Wood’s own request. He asked that anyone who tried to decipher his message “communicate the result (even if negative) to the Society”.

He promised the Society “fuller particulars” about himself and photographs of him and his home, and said his executors would be asked to tell it “the circumstances” and date of his death.

The Society’s archive officer replied to my email on October 5, 2026, confirming that Wood’s file is held at Cambridge University Library and offering to include the result in a Psi Encyclopedia article on ciphers. I have also asked the library about the file and how to obtain copies.
